    Sources & methodology

    Every figure below traces to a named institutional source. Where the map renders a spatial pattern rather than a precise gridded dataset, or a scenario tool extrapolates from documented history, that is stated explicitly.

    ENSO / El Niño & La Niña

    • NOAA Climate Prediction Center — Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), Niño 3.4 region; category thresholds: weak 0.5–0.9°C, moderate 1.0–1.4°C, strong 1.5–1.9°C, very strong ≥2.0°C.
    • Feb 2026: NOAA/NWS transitioned from ONI to the Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI), which subtracts the tropical-mean SST trend (NWS Public Information Statement 26-05).
    • Historic peaks: 1982–83 (RONI 2.69°C), 1997–98, 2015–16 (ONI ≈2.6°C). IRI's Aug 2026 outlook flagged the 2026–27 event as potentially record-breaking (Niño 3.4 ≥2.0–3.0°C across most models).
    • This atlas expresses intensity at a fixed 0.5°C-per-SD scale so NOAA's category bands land on whole units (1–4 SD); 5–6 SD extends the scale for exceptional events. This is the app's own display convention, not a published statistical SD.
    • Regional teleconnection shading follows NOAA/IRI's published ENSO impact composites.

    Global temperature

    • NASA GISS (GISTEMP v4): 2025 anomaly +1.19°C, 2024 +1.28°C (warmest on record), vs. the 1951–1980 baseline; every year since 2015 ranks among the ten warmest on record.
    • Polar amplification: Arctic warming has proceeded at roughly 3–4× the global average (NOAA Arctic Report Card; IPCC AR6 WGI Ch.2). The map's latitude gradient illustrates this documented ratio; it is not gridded satellite data.

    Sea-level rise

    • IPCC AR6 (2021), vs. 1995–2014: by 2100, 0.28 m (SSP1-1.9) to 0.63–1.01 m (SSP5-8.5); a low-confidence ice-sheet-instability storyline allows "approaching 2 m by 2100 and 5 m by 2150" under very high emissions; by 2300, 0.3–3.1 m to 1.7–6.8 m.
    • Lancet Countdown 2020: 145–565 million people currently live in zones facing potential inundation from sea-level rise, depending on scenario.
    • Coastal markers flag documented low-elevation, high-exposure deltas and cities from IPCC AR6 WGII and NOAA/Climate Central low-elevation coastal-zone research — shown as risk zones, not a precise bathtub-flood model.

    Land degradation & desertification

    • UNCCD (2024): up to 40% of the planet's land is degraded, affecting roughly half of humanity and an estimated US$44 trillion of global GDP; at least 100 million hectares of healthy land lost every year, 2015–2019.
    • Droughts have grown 29% more frequent and severe since 2000 (UNCCD COP16, 2024).
    • FAO (2015) estimated 24% of global land was degraded between 1981–2003; the app interpolates a simple, clearly-labeled trend line between that figure and UNCCD's 2024 "up to 40%" figure — an illustrative interpolation between two survey points, not an annual dataset.
    • Regional hotspots drawn from UNCCD's Global Land Outlook 2 (2024).

    Economic cost of climate & weather disasters

    • NOAA NCEI Billion-Dollar Weather & Climate Disasters (U.S., inflation-adjusted, 1980–2024): tropical cyclones total $1,543.2B (avg. $23.0B/event, 67 events, 7,211 deaths); severe storms $514.3B ($2.5B avg., 203 events — the most frequent type); drought $367.5B (assessed at seasonal/regional scale); inland flooding $203.0B ($4.7B avg., 45 events); Western wildfires alone exceeded $90B in 2017–2021 vs. $58B for all other wildfire events combined since 1980.
    • 2024 U.S. total: 27 billion-dollar disasters, $182.7B in damage, 568 deaths — Hurricane Helene was the costliest single event.
    • Munich Re: global natural-catastrophe losses reached $320B in 2024 (fifth-costliest year since 1980), of which $140B was insured; weather-related events caused 93% of overall losses. 2024 named events: Hurricane Helene ~$56B, Hurricane Milton $38B ($25B insured), Valencia, Spain floods $11B (deadliest European natural disaster in 50 years).
    • Historic El Niño costs: 1997–98 caused an estimated $5.7T in cumulative global income loss over five years (Callahan & Mankin, Science, 2023); a separate macroeconomic model attributes $2.1T (1997–98) and $3.9T (2015–16) in global losses (Nature Communications, 2023).

    AI & the labor market

    • World Economic Forum, Future of Jobs Report 2025: 92 million jobs displaced and 170 million created globally by 2030 (net +78 million; 22% structural churn across the 1.2 billion formal jobs surveyed, from 1,000+ employers across 55 economies).
    • Goldman Sachs Research: ~300 million full-time-equivalent jobs globally are exposed to generative AI; generative AI could raise global GDP by ~7% (~US$7 trillion) over 10 years; roughly 2.5% of U.S. employment faces direct displacement risk, 6–7% (~11 million workers) broader task-transformation risk.
    • IMF AI Preparedness Index: North America scores highest (0.74); Sub-Saharan Africa (0.34) and low-income countries generally (0.32) are the least prepared for the transition.
    • PwC 2025 Global AI Jobs Barometer: workers with AI skills earn a 56% wage premium over peers in the same role without them.
    • McKinsey (2025): up to 375 million workers globally (~14% of the global workforce) may need to switch occupational categories by 2030.
    • Occupation-level exposure scores: Wharton Budget Model 2025 AI-exposure dataset (784 occupations), as reported by EDsmart, Dec 2025; and the AgentLocker AI Jobs Automation Index, 2025. Lowest-exposure occupations: US Career Institute occupational analysis, 2025; Pew Research Center, as cited in DesignRush, 2026.

    Health & disease

    • Lancet Countdown 2024 report: climatic suitability for dengue transmission rose 46.3% (Aedes albopictus) and 10.7% (Aedes aegypti) between 1951–60 and 2014–23.
    • Lancet Countdown 2021 report: the number of months suitable for malaria transmission in densely populated highland areas of low-HDI countries rose 39% between 1950–59 and 2010–19.
    • Lancet Countdown 2020: global yield potential for major staple crops declined 1.8–5.6% between 1981 and 2019.

    The Predictor tool

    • The predictor interpolates between the documented historical anchor points above (e.g. the 1997–98 and 2015–16 El Niño food-security and cost figures, UNCCD's land-loss rate, Lancet Countdown's disease-suitability trend) using a simple, transparent scaling function shown in full inside the tool.
    • It is an illustrative extrapolation for exploring how documented relationships might scale — not a climate, economic, or epidemiological forecast from a scientific model.
